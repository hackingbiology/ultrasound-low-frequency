Testing path

One hand treated, the other one watching

Before anyone fills a bathtub, there is a much better experiment available: treat one hand, keep the other as a control, and measure the difference. It is cheaper, far safer, and — because the control lives on the same body — it is scientifically stronger than anything the whole-body version can do.

Why this comes first. Not merely because it is safer, though it is. Because a contralateral control removes almost every confounder that makes n-of-1 self-experimentation unreliable, and because ultrasound at this intensity is imperceptible, which means you can genuinely blind yourself.

Why a limb beats a tub, scientifically

The contralateral control

The treated hand and the control hand share a body: the same sleep, diet, infections, stress, season, medication, and the same assay batch on the same day. Every confounder that forced the blood-marker plan into three baselines and made IL-6 and hs-CRP unusable simply cancels out.

This is the oldest trick in dermatology and in sports medicine, and it is unavailable to a whole-body protocol by construction.

Real blinding, for free

At 4–8 kPa the exposure is inaudible and cannot be felt — which is precisely how the UT Health trial blinds its participants to the sham. Two identical basins at the same temperature, the transducer energised in one, the controller out of sight, and someone else tossing a coin for which side: you do not know which hand is being treated.

Add blinded assessment — photographs and measurements labelled by a third party — and this becomes a randomised, sham-controlled, within-person crossover. That is a real design.

And skin is the one tissue you can actually watch. You will never see the kidney or the pancreas that the mouse study measured. You can see skin every day — and skin is exactly where senolytic trials went looking: in the dasatinib-plus-quercetin diabetic-kidney-disease study, epidermal p16 and p21 fell by 38% and 30%. Mechanobiologics' own wellness claim is "enhancing skin thickness, improving elasticity". So a hand protocol measures precisely what the originators say their device does.

The study design

  1. Randomise the side. Someone else flips the coin and sets up the basins; they keep the allocation. You record which basin is which only by a neutral label.
  2. Sham that is a real sham. Both hands sit in identical basins, same water, same temperature, same duration. Only one basin has the transducer driven. The warm-water effect — which is real and would otherwise be mistaken for the treatment — is applied to both sides.
  3. Baseline in triplicate. Three measurement sessions before starting, so you know your own measurement error and can compute what change would count as real.
  4. Treat, then wash out, then cross over. After the treatment block and a washout, swap sides and repeat. If the effect follows the hand rather than the side, that is far stronger evidence than any single block.
  5. Blinded assessment. Photographs and measurements are coded by the third party and analysed without knowing which side is which.
  6. Pre-register and publish. Declare the primary endpoint before starting, then publish the result whichever way it falls — and in a format that lets several people's runs be pooled later.

Blood is not the endpoint here. A hand is a tiny fraction of body mass; expecting plasma GDF-15 to move is unrealistic. Draw and freeze samples anyway if you are already set up for it — but the result of this study lives in the hand, not in the serum.

Paired 3 mm punch biopsies with a dermatologist (p16, p21, SA-β-gal, collagen on treated vs control hand) remain the gold-standard endpoint and the only direct measure of senescent burden. They are deliberately out of scope for now and can be added later without changing anything else in the design.

The rig

  • Two identical basins, 5–15 litres. Plastic, non-conductive. One holds the transducer; the other is identical in every visible respect.
  • This is the only volume in the whole project that can genuinely be degassed — boiling and cooling sealed, or a vacuum chamber in two batches — and degassing is the entire cavitation-safety argument. Measure dissolved oxygen, target ≤2 mg/L.
  • Geometry fixed by a jig: a 3-D-printed or wooden rest that puts the hand at the same height, 7–10 cm above the transducer face, in the same position every session. Mark it.
  • Dose: the standard target, 32–33 kHz, 4–8 kPa measured at the hand position, 1.5 s on / 1.5 s off, 30 minutes. Mapped once with the hydrophone, then monitored every session by the sentinel.
  • Electrically: battery-powered, floating, extra-low voltage along the cable, step-up sealed inside the potted head — same rules as the home retrofit, but with your head metres away from the water and a hand you can withdraw in a second.
  • Thermal: both basins at the same temperature, 35–37 °C, logged. A temperature difference between the two sides would ruin the comparison more thoroughly than any acoustic error.

Risks specific to a hand

  • Skin maceration from repeated 30-minute immersions — inspect both hands at every session, dry thoroughly, and pause if the skin is breaking down. Note that this affects both hands equally, which is part of why the control matters.
  • Nerves and tendons are superficial in the hand. Any numbness, tingling, burning or new joint pain on the treated side is a stop signal, not a side effect to push through. The vibration and two-point tests exist to catch this before you notice it.
  • Exclude the hand if it contains implants, plates, screws, or has had recent surgery; exclude anyone with a wound, active infection or skin disease on either hand.
  • Electrical: a hand in the water is far less dangerous than a torso, but it is still a conductive path. Battery power, extra-low voltage, insulation check before each session.
  • Hearing: the risk that dominates the whole-body version largely disappears here, because your head is not coupled to the water. Keep it that way — no leaning over the basin for long periods.
  • The general risks of the exposure itself, including the oncogenic question, still apply — but to a few hundred grams of tissue instead of a whole body.

What it can and cannot prove

What it answers

  • Does anything measurable happen at all, in a real tissue, beyond placebo and beyond warm water?
  • Does the effect follow the treated side, and does it reverse after washout and crossover?
  • Is our delivered dose reproducible session after session?
  • Does the exposure cause any local harm?

What it does not answer

  • Anything systemic. A hand is a tiny fraction of body mass, and blood markers almost certainly will not move.
  • Whether senescent burden fell — that needs tissue, i.e. the biopsies we have deliberately deferred.
  • Whether the whole-body protocol is safe; the hearing and electrical questions scale with immersion, not with dose.

The asymmetry is what makes it worth doing. If a blinded, contralateral-controlled hand study shows nothing across two blocks and a crossover, that is a serious negative result about an effect that is claimed to be large — obtained with ten litres of water instead of two hundred, and without anyone being immersed. If it shows something, you have the first independently measured, dose-documented human signal for this intervention, and a design that other builders can repeat exactly.
