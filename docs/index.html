HackingBiology · open hardware · DIY biohacking

Low-frequency ultrasound, built by biohackers and measured with blood tests

An open, reproducible, self-buildable version of the ~33 kHz immersion-ultrasound device from University of Texas mechanobiology research. The goal is a device that other people can build and calibrate themselves, and then use to measure its biological effects on their own bodies.

Living research notes · started 2026-09-16 · GitHub

What this is and is not.
  • It is a non-commercial DIY biohacking project. We document how to design, build, calibrate and self-test a research device, and we share the data openly.
  • It is not a medical device, a treatment or a product. It makes no therapeutic claim, and nothing is sold.
  • The evidence so far is preclinical: cells and mice from a single lab. The long-term safety of "reversing" senescence in humans is unknown. Read DIY biohacking: benefits & risks before anything else.

The idea in one minute

In 2022–2025 the late Michael Sheetz's group reported something striking. A weak pulse of low-frequency ultrasound, delivered through water, made senescent human cells start dividing again. Their senescence markers dropped (SA-β-gal, p16/p21, SASP cytokines). Old mice given repeated sessions were far more active, and they had fewer senescence-marker-positive cells in kidney and pancreas. The work is now owned by Mechanobiologics Inc. and is being tested in a sham-controlled human "ultrasound bathtub" trial at UT Health San Antonio (XPRIZE Healthspan).

The physics is surprisingly accessible. The stimulus is about 0.5–4 mW/cm², which is thousands of times weaker than an ultrasonic cleaner. The component family is the same as in cleaning baths. The hard part is not generating 33 kHz. It is proving the dose, meaning a known, uniform, non-cavitating pressure field at the body, measured by someone else's hydrophone and not just by the knob setting.

Project goals

  1. Reproduce the dose, not the box. Write an open, measurable definition of the exposure: frequency, pressure (with its peak/RMS convention), duty cycle, spatial uniformity and cavitation limits. Define an acceptance test that any build must pass.
  2. Reference prototype. Build a first calibrated unit using the three hardware architectures we are evaluating, with the lowest cost and skill barrier compatible with a verified dose.
  3. Make self-built kits easy to adopt. Publish a bill of materials with alternates, open PCB/firmware/3D files, a step-by-step build guide, a shared calibration workflow (a circulating reference hydrophone plus DIY transfer hydrophones) and a public registry of verified builds.
  4. Measure biology, properly. A self-testing protocol with multiple baselines, a sham phase, and blood and functional markers matched to the UT human trial. See Analyze biological feedback.
  5. Stay safe and legal. Electrical safety for water immersion, oncogenic-risk awareness, exclusion criteria, and a clear boundary on what a non-commercial DIY project may do under the patents. See IP rights.

From the first build to many self-built kits

Every design decision is judged by one question: can the second, tenth and hundredth builder reproduce the result without us?

1 · Reference build

One unit, fully characterised: a 3-D pressure map, a cavitation check, thermal and electrical safety. Every measurement is published as raw data.

2 · Builder kit

Off-the-shelf parts with EU/CN alternates, open PCB (KiCad), MicroPython/C firmware, 3-D-printable fixtures, a build guide and a tool list. No custom machining.

3 · Verify & register

Each new build runs the acceptance test with a calibrated or transfer hydrophone and publishes its calibration file. Only verified builds contribute biological data.
